\chapter{Conclusion}
The application of reinforcement learning to real-world problems remains challenging. As discussed earlier, several approaches, including robust, model-based, and offline reinforcement learning, look to overcome these difficulties in a variety of ways. Whether the goal is to increase sample efficiency through learned models, reduce the cost of data collection with offline optimization, or account for uncertainty during the learning process, these methods help mitigate many of the problems associated with physical deployment. However, each approach also introduces its own limitations and trade-offs.

The proposed approach combines ideas from each of these methods, aiming to leverage their strengths while compensating for their weaknesses. First, a data collection strategy is employed to build an initial dataset of context-action-reward tuples. This strategy incorporates samples from an expert policy and an exploration policy, combining the high-reward behavior of the expert with the broad action coverage provided by exploration to produce a diverse dataset. Then, a reward model is trained on the data, represented by an ensemble of networks. The ensemble outputs reward predictions while incorporating an uncertainty penalty, producing more conservative reward estimates in regions where the model is less certain. Next, a robust optimization objective, named the PAR objective, is used to optimize a policy. This objective produces policies that trade off robustness and performance, leading to high-reward actions that remain resilient to perturbations. Finally, the method integrates iterative policy deployment and data aggregation, followed by retraining of the reward model and policy. This process improves model accuracy in relevant regions and yields better policies over time. Together, these components provide a practical framework for applying reinforcement learning in real-world settings.

To evaluate the proposed method, experiments were conducted in simulated and physical settings against two robust reinforcement learning baselines. In the Buffalo Gym environment, the objective comparison showed that the PAR objective achieved the best performance under noisy conditions. Furthermore, the sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the approach remains stable across a range of values for the uncertainty coefficient $\beta$. Finally, the objectives were evaluated on the robotic sorting task, where the PAR objective again outperformed all methods across all scenarios. Collectively, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach and its ability to address the challenges of real-world reinforcement learning.

\section{Limitations}
Although this method provides an effective framework for real-world reinforcement learning, several limitations remain. Even with the proposed data collection strategy, acquiring real-world data is still expensive and time consuming. As a result, obtaining sufficient data becomes increasingly challenging as tasks become more complex. Additionally, while the use of an approximate reward model is more realistic than assuming access to the true reward function, model inaccuracies can negatively affect performance. Because the policy is optimized with respect to the learned reward model, prediction errors may compound during optimization and lead to suboptimal behavior. In the robotic setup, the discretized context simplifies learning by drastically reducing the size of the context space, but it also provides an incomplete representation of the environment. Important factors such as the number of items, object deformation and occlusion are omitted, limiting the agent's ability to fully understand the task. Finally, the contextual bandit formulation has no notion of time and instead focuses solely on the immediate reward. This shrinks the application of the approach to relatively simple settings, as it cannot account for the effects of current actions on future rewards.

\section{Future Work}
The work in this paper has the potential to be extended in several future directions. First, it would be valuable to extend this approach from the contextual bandit setting to the Markov decision process framework. This would allow the method to be applied to more complex sequential decision-making tasks where long-term consequences must be considered. Additionally, incorporating the robotic setup into a simulation environment like Gazebo or MuJoCo would allow experimentation with larger state and action spaces and enable the use of more data-intensive learning methods. Transfer learning techniques could then be used to deploy policies learned in simulation on the real robot. Finally, future work could investigate the use of stochastic policies rather than deterministic ones. This could lead to improved exploration during training and the development of more adaptable policies.
